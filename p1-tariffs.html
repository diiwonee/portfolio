01

Project Overview

This project examines two tariffs connected to Kazakhstan: the US 50% tariff on copper imports and Kazakhstan's own Progressive Export Customs Duty on crude oil. Both were assigned as examples of "ridiculous" tariffs — instruments that, despite their stated purpose, produce outcomes that undermine their own goals.

The activity challenged us to look beyond the surface reason for a tariff (usually "national security" or "protecting industry") and ask: what does this tariff actually do? The answers were far more political, and far more ironic, than we expected.

The Copper Paradox

The US produces 5% of the world's copper — then taxes 100% of its imports at 50%

The US needs copper for weapons, EVs, and electronics. Imposing a 50% tariff doesn't create domestic mines overnight (that takes 10+ years). It just makes everything more expensive — and quietly pushes Kazakhstan, which can't negotiate independently due to EAEU membership, to sell to China instead. The policy intended to weaken Russia's bloc may actually strengthen it.

Why it exists

National security framing — copper is critical for defence and green energy. Also part of broader US-China trade war strategy to reduce dependency on Eurasian partners.

Actual effect

Kazakhstan's copper exporters lose US market access or absorb the cost. Building new US mines takes 10+ years. No quick domestic production increase.

The irony

Kazakhstan, unable to negotiate bilaterally (EAEU rules require permission from Russia, Belarus, Armenia, Kyrgyzstan), quietly redirects exports to China — strengthening the bloc the US wants to weaken.

Kazakhstan's oil export duty is "progressive" — meaning the more expensive oil gets globally, the higher the export tax. It's essentially a success tax.

The Oil Paradox

The National Fund grows. The citizen's purchasing power shrinks.

The progressive ECD fills the government's budget during high oil prices — exactly when Kazakhstan's exporters are most competitive. But to offset the tax, fuel subsidies are removed. Combined with the new 16% VAT (up from 12%), transport costs rise, food prices rise, and inflation sits at 11.7% (Feb 2026). The government's wealth is directly funded by citizen cost-of-living.

Evidence from Coursework

This project was completed as a group assignment with Zhaniya (ID 2417691). Below are key facts and data points from our research document.

US Production
The US produces approximately 5% of global copper — making it highly import-dependent even while imposing a 50% tariff on copper imports.
EAEU Constraint
Kazakhstan's EAEU membership means it cannot negotiate bilateral trade deals independently. Any response to the US tariff requires agreement from Russia, Belarus, Armenia, and Kyrgyzstan.
Oil Duty Rate
In March 2026, Kazakhstan's oil export duty was set at $69 per ton. At prices above $105/bbl, the duty jumps to $236/ton.
Inflation Impact
Kazakhstan's inflation was recorded at 11.7% (February 2026), partly driven by the removal of fuel subsidies and a VAT increase from 12% to 16%.
04

What Did I Learn?

Knowledge
Tariffs are often more political than economic. A country can use an export duty as a "wealth grab" from its own industries. The EAEU structure fundamentally limits Kazakhstan's trade autonomy.
Skills
Research, data interpretation, critical analysis of stated policy goals vs. actual effects. Learning to trace second-order consequences (tariff → redirect → geopolitical strengthening of rival bloc).
Reflection
What surprised me most: being a resource-rich country isn't straightforwardly good. Kazakhstan's oil wealth is captured by a tax that then raises prices for the very citizens it's supposed to benefit. The system creates wealth at the top while quietly transferring cost downward.
