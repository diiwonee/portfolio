The Pizza Optimiser —
Linear Programming

How many of each pizza to produce to maximise profit? A deceptively simple question that opens the door to linear optimisation.

01

Project Overview

The Pizza Optimiser was our introduction to mathematical modelling and linear programming. The activity asked: given two types of pizza with different profits, ingredient costs, and labour requirements — and limited resources — how many of each should you produce to maximise profit?

This led us to formalise the problem using proper LP notation from Chapter 4: defining decision variables, an objective function, parameters, sets, and constraints. The beauty of LP is that once you write it down formally, the math finds the answer.

Standard LP Notation — Pizza Problem

Sets 𝑃 = {Margherita, Pepperoni} — the set of pizza types
Decision Variables x₁ = units of Margherita produced  |  x₂ = units of Pepperoni produced
Objective Function (maximise profit) max Z = p₁x₁ + p₂x₂
Subject to Constraints Dough: d₁x₁ + d₂x₂ ≤ D_max
Labour: l₁x₁ + l₂x₂ ≤ L_max
Non-negativity: x₁, x₂ ≥ 0

03

Evidence from Coursework

The Pizza Optimiser was an in-class activity followed by a custom optimisation problem assignment. We used AI to generate five similar LP problems and selected one to solve with proper LP notation.

Activity
Completed the Pizza Optimiser document in class. Defined each part with LP notation from Chapter 4: sets, parameters, decision variables, objective function, constraints.
Assignment
Used AI to generate 5 custom LP problems based on the Pizza Optimiser structure. Selected one, solved it in a Google Sheet, and submitted with full notation and linked AI conversation.
Key insight
The objective function is always linear — profit per unit × quantity. The constraints form a feasible region. The optimal solution always lies at a corner (vertex) of that region. Once you see it geometrically, it clicks.
04

What Did I Learn?

Knowledge
Linear programming structure: sets, parameters, decision variables, objective function, constraints. The concept of the feasible region and why the optimal solution is always at a vertex.
Skills
Translating a word problem into LP notation. Using spreadsheet solvers. Prompting AI to generate parallel problems. Verifying solutions against constraints manually.
Reflection
The hardest part was the notation — it felt overly formal for what seemed like a simple problem. But once I saw that the notation is what lets computers solve problems with hundreds of variables, it made sense. The formalism is the point.
