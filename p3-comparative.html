01

Project Overview

This project covers two major trade models studied in class: the Ricardian model (trade based on labour productivity differences) and the Heckscher-Ohlin model (trade based on factor endowments — land, labour, capital). Both models answer the question: why do countries trade? — but they give fundamentally different answers.

The activity involved a comparative advantage spreadsheet exercise and reading chapters on both models. This page brings those concepts to life with an interactive calculator and PPF visualiser.

03

Evidence from Coursework

This project draws from the comparative advantage spreadsheet activity (class exercise) and Readings 2 and 3 (Chapters 3–5 of the textbook). Key guiding questions addressed:

GQ 1
How does H-O differ from Ricardian? The Ricardian model assumes only labour matters and that technology differs. H-O assumes technology is identical across countries but factor endowments differ — the ratio of capital to labour, or land to labour, determines what a country exports.
GQ 2
Factor price equalisation. H-O predicts that as countries trade, wages and capital returns converge globally. In practice, this doesn't fully happen: transport costs, trade barriers, and non-tradeable services prevent full equalisation. Highly skilled vs. unskilled labour may face different dynamics.
GQ 3
How can a weak economy trade with a superpower? Comparative advantage shows that even if Country A is worse at producing everything, it still has a relative advantage in the good where its disadvantage is smallest. Trade benefits both. Total output increases. This was Ricardo's counterintuitive, powerful insight.
04

What Did I Learn?

Knowledge
The difference between absolute and comparative advantage. The Ricardian model's elegant but oversimplified logic. H-O's more realistic but harder-to-test framework. Factor price equalisation as theory vs. reality.
Skills
Spreadsheet modelling, opportunity cost calculations, drawing and interpreting PPF diagrams, reading economic research papers and extracting key arguments.
Reflection
The most surprising thing: comparative advantage feels impossible until you do the math. The idea that a less productive country should still trade — and that both sides gain — seems wrong intuitively. The model reveals something real that instinct misses. That's what makes it valuable.
