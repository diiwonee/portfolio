International Trade · Project 04

Middleman Risks:
A Breaking Bad Analogy

How does a supply chain intermediary work — and what can a fictional drug empire teach us about trade, margins, and systemic risk?

01

Project Overview

In international trade, intermediaries (middlemen) play a crucial role: they connect producers to buyers across borders, absorb information costs, manage risk, and add value through logistics and relationships. But middlemen also concentrate risk — and create fragility when their chain breaks.

Breaking Bad's supply chain — from Heisenberg (producer) through distributors to street-level dealers — maps surprisingly well onto a real-world trade intermediary model. Each layer adds margin, absorbs risk, and is vulnerable to disruption at its specific point in the chain. This project uses that analogy to explain how intermediary risk works in global trade.

Heisenberg (Producer)
Exporter / Manufacturer

Produces the good. Has specialised knowledge and production capacity. Dependent on the distribution chain to reach the market — cannot access end buyers directly. Risk: if the chain breaks above him, he has no route to market.

Gus Fring (Distributor)
Regional Intermediary

Controls the logistics, relationships, and route to market. Adds value through information, network access, and risk management. Has significant leverage over the producer — and is the single point of failure if disrupted. Classic middleman position.

Street Dealers
Wholesalers / Retailers

Final distribution layer before the end consumer. Absorb local market risk. Have direct knowledge of demand conditions. Most exposed to enforcement (regulatory) risk — the equivalent of tariffs, customs inspections, and local regulation.

The DEA
Trade Barriers / Regulators

External enforcement mechanism that raises the cost of doing business, creates compliance costs, and periodically disrupts the chain entirely. Equivalent to tariffs, customs duties, sanctions, and regulatory barriers in international trade.

03

Evidence & Analysis

Core concept
Intermediaries add value by reducing transaction costs — the costs of finding buyers, negotiating, and enforcing contracts across borders. Without them, producers and consumers couldn't connect efficiently across distance and information gaps.
The risk
Each intermediary layer creates a single point of failure. When Gus is eliminated, the whole chain collapses — not because production stops, but because the distribution network is severed. Same in trade: a key importer going bankrupt, a port closing, or a regulatory change can halt an entire supply chain.
Margin capture
The longer the chain, the larger the gap between producer price and consumer price — and the smaller the producer's share. This is a real tension in global commodity trade: coffee farmers may receive 1-3% of the retail price of a cup of coffee.
04

What Did I Learn?

Knowledge
Transaction cost theory. The role of information asymmetry in trade intermediation. How chain length relates to price markup and producer vulnerability.
Skills
Applying an analogy from popular culture to abstract economic concepts. Building a quantitative model from a qualitative idea. Systems thinking across a supply chain.
Reflection
The Breaking Bad analogy worked better than expected — not because the show is about economics, but because the writers understood the logic of value chains and power dynamics intuitively. Good storytelling often encodes economic logic. The difficult part was not making it silly — keeping the analysis serious even with a dramatic frame.
