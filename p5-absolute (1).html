01

Project Overview

This project addresses a foundational question in trade theory: what determines whether a country should produce a good or import it? The answer depends not on whether you're the best at producing it (absolute advantage), but on your relative efficiency (comparative advantage).

The guiding questions from Reading 2 (Chapters 3–4) asked us to define the fundamental difference between absolute and comparative advantage, explain how weak economies can still benefit from trade, and interpret the PPF model visually.

Concept 1

Absolute Advantage

Country A has absolute advantage in a good if it can produce it using fewer resources than Country B. Simple and intuitive — but it's NOT what determines trade patterns.

Concept 2 — The key one

Comparative Advantage

Country A has comparative advantage in a good if its opportunity cost of producing it is lower than Country B's. This determines specialisation and trade — even if A is worse at everything.

03

Evidence from Coursework

Reading 2
Chapters 3–4 of the textbook. Key insight from the Ricardian model: specialisation according to comparative advantage maximises total output for both countries. Even if Country A is better at everything, trade still benefits both.
Norman's Question
Class question from Norman: "How can a less productive country possibly compete with a superpower?" The Ricardian answer: it doesn't need to compete in every good — only in the good where its relative disadvantage is smallest. That good is where its comparative advantage lies.
PPF Shape
A linear PPF (as in the Ricardian model) reflects constant opportunity costs. A bowed-out PPF reflects increasing opportunity costs as resources are shifted. Both show the fundamental trade-off between producing two goods with limited resources.

What Did I Learn?

Knowledge
The difference between absolute and comparative advantage. Opportunity cost as the real measure of trade efficiency. How Ricardo's model proves that even a less productive country gains from trade — not charity, but mutual benefit.
Skills
Calculating opportunity costs from production data. Reading and drawing PPF diagrams. Translating numerical tables into trade policy recommendations.
Reflection
This concept felt impossible until I did the calculation. Comparative advantage is one of those economic ideas that genuinely changes how you see the world — trade isn't a zero-sum competition where the strong always win. The weak country gains too. That surprised me. It probably shouldn't have — Ricardo proved it in 1817 — but it did.
